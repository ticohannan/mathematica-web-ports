### [Title]
Shortest Path for the Dubins Car

### [Section]
Caption<<PaneSelectorBox>>

### [Text]
In 1957, Lester Eli Dubins proved that the shortest path between two (x,y,Theta) coordinates for a forward-moving vehicle with a minimum turning radius Subscript[r, min] is composed entirely of no more than three circular arcs of radius Subscript[r, min] or straight lines.

### [Text]
In this Demonstration, the starting position of the blue car is green and its end position (the goal) is red. You can drag two locators, one for the red car's (x,y) position and the other for the red car's orientation Theta. The sliders let you vary the minimum turning radius Subscript[r, min] or move the blue car from the start to the goal.

angPt2Pt[ps_,pe_]:=Chop[N[ArcTan[pe[[1]]-ps[[1]],pe[[2]]-ps[[2]]]]];
distPt2Pt[ps_,pe_]:=Sqrt[(pe[[1]]-ps[[1]])^2+(pe[[2]]-ps[[2]])^2];

arcLen[aS_,aE_,dir_]:=Module[{Theta,a1,a2},
a1=aS;a2 = aE;Theta = a2-a1;
While[dir>0\[And]Theta<0,Theta+=2π;a2+=2π;];
While[dir<0\[And]Theta>0,Theta-=2π;a2-=2π;];

If[Theta>2π,Theta -=2π;a2-=2π];
If[Theta<-2π,Theta+=2π;a2+=2π];
{Abs[Theta],a1,a2}]

drawLSL[csL_,ceL_,Theta_,minRadius_]:=Module[{oLSL,dLSL,dLL = distPt2Pt[csL,ceL],AlphaL2L =angPt2Pt[csL,ceL ],arc1,arc2},

arc1 = arcLen[-π/2,AlphaL2L -π/2,1];
arc2= arcLen[AlphaL2L -π/2,Theta-π/2,1];
dLSL = dLL+minRadius*(arc1 [[1]]+ arc2[[1]]);
{dLSL,
{Pink,
Circle[csL,minRadius,arc1[[2;;3]]],
Line[{oLSL+csL,oLSL+ceL}],
Circle[ceL,minRadius,arc2[[2;;3]]]}}
]

### [Text]
The Dubins car is a simplified mathematical model of a car that moves in the x-y plane[1]. The car's location is specified by the (x,y) location of the center of the car's rear axle and the orientation Theta of the car. The car cannot move sideways because the rear wheels would have to slide instead of roll. The Dubins car model stipulates that the car moves forward at a constant speed and has a maximum steering angle that translates into a minimum turning radius Subscript[r, min]. The minimum turning radius circles are drawn tangent to the starting and ending positions with dashed gray circles.

### [Text]
\<If the car has forward velocity of 1 unit per second, the system equations are\>

### [Text]
dx=cos Theta, 
dy=sin Theta,
dTheta=u,

### [Text]
where u∈[-1/Subscript[r, min],1/Subscript[r, min]]. The task is to minimize the length of the curve traced out by the center of the rear axle as it moves from the start to the goal. That path is the bounded-curvature shortest path. Dubins proved that the shortest path consists of no more than three segments, where in each segment u is either -1/Subscript[r, min], 0 or 1/Subscript[r, min]. Denote these steering commands by R, S, L for turning right, going straight or turning left. A path is then coded with a three-letter word.

### [Text]
If Subscript[r, min]=0, then the shortest path is a straight line. For nonzero Subscript[r, min], the shortest paths are of the form LRL, RLR, LSL, LSR, RSL, RSR. In this Demonstration, each path type is given a different color: LRL is brown, RLR is dark green, LSL is pink, LSR is blue, RSL is orange, and RSR is dark yellow.

### [Text]
LSR (blue) and RSL (orange) are potentially valid only if the distance from start to goal is greater than 2 Subscript[r, min]. The two paths composed entirely of curves, LRL (brown) and RLR (dark green), are potentially valid only if the distance from start to goal is less than 4 Subscript[r, min].

### [Text]
\<This Demonstration determines the shortest path by computing the paths using all of the valid words, and then picks the one with shortest length.\>

### [Text]
\<All paths are drawn in their respective colors, but the shortest path is drawn with a thicker line. Techniques in[4] show how to determine the regions where each path is optimal. If the car is allowed to move backward as well as forward, there are 46 possible words, and this mathematical model is called the Reeds\[Dash]Shepp car[2].\>
