### [Title]
Robot Motion with Obstacles

### [Section]
Caption<<PaneSelectorBox>>

### [Text]
This Demonstration shows a two-link planar arm robot operating in the workspace on the left, containing two small circular obstacles. The corresponding robot phase space on the right shows the configurations q=(Subscript[Theta, 1],Subscript[Theta, 2]) for which an arm intersects an obstacle. You can relocate an obstacle in the workspace and see the corresponding change in phase space. Move the locator in phase space vertically or horizontally to rotate the robot arms. The angles Subscript[Theta, 1] and Subscript[Theta, 2] are expressed modulo 2π. The obstacle regions are computed using 32Q unit squares. You can improve the quality of the computation by increasing Q.

### [Input] (init)
(*returns the shortest distance between line segment 'seg' and a list of points*)
pointsegdis2[{seg_,pointlist_}]:=Module[{u=Subtract@@seg,mean=Plus@@seg/2},Min[EuclideanDistance[#,(mean-u Sign@# Min[1,Abs@#]/2)&@(-(2 (#-mean).u)/u.u)]&/@pointlist]];
isCollided[{seg_,pointlist_}, d_]:=Module[{u=Subtract@@seg,mean=Plus@@seg/2},If[(Min[EuclideanDistance[#,(mean-u Sign@# Min[1,Abs@#]/2)&@(-(2 (#-mean).u)/u.u)]&/@pointlist]<d),1,0]];
(*to space the overlay plot*)
xMin = 2.9;yMin =-2;xMax=xMin+3.8;yMax=yMin+3.8;

### [Text]
The set of all possible configurations of the robot can be represented in a two-dimensional phase space. Of particular interest are the configurations in which the robot collides with one of the obstacles.

### [Text]
This Demonstration computes the obstacle regions using 32Q unit squares and determines if the centroid of each square is in a collision. Given Subscript[Theta, 1] and Subscript[Theta, 2], the motion computation is trivial.

### [Text]
\<However, computing the phase-space obstacle region is more difficult, because the size of the space representation grows exponentially with the number of degrees of freedom (independent joints) of the robot.\>

### [Text]
Consider how many n-dimensional unit squares are required to fill a space of size k. For a one-dimensional space, k^1 units are needed. For a two-dimensional space, k^2 squares are needed. For an n-dimensional space, k^n hypercubes would be required.

### [Text]
The phase space for a two-link planar robot can be represented on a torus. When rotation joint 1 moves past 2π, it returns to angle 0. This is represented by dashed and solid lines of the same color. If the phase space were printed on a rubber sheet, cut out, and the dashed and solid lines of each color were joined, the resulting shape would be a torus. In this Demonstration, moving the locator past the edge of the phase space wraps the angle around 2π.
