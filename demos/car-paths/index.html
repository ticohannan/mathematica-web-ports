Shortest Path for Forward and Reverse Motion of a Car

This Demonstration shows the shortest paths for a car in forward and reverse motion, with a minimum turning radius rmin. Use the locators to change the start and goal positions and orientations. A car with both forward and reverse gears is often called a Reeds–Shepp car, while one with only a forward gear is a Dubins car, in honor of the mathematicians who first studied these problems.

Details

This Demonstration is based on a simplified mathematical model of a car that can move in the x-y plane [1]. The car's coordinates are the center of the rear axle (x, y) and its orientation θ. The car cannot move sideways because the rear wheels would have to slide instead of roll. The model stipulates that the car has a maximum steering angle that translates into a minimum turning radius rmin. Circles showing the minimum turning radius are drawn as thin gray lines tangent to the curved sections of the path.

The changes of coordinates with velocity v are given by

dx = v cos θ,
dy = v sin θ,
dθ = u,

with the steering constraint limits u ∈ [−1/rmin, 1/rmin]. The task is to minimize the length of the curve traced out by the center of the rear axle as it moves from the start to the goal [2]. That path is the bounded-curvature shortest path. Reeds and Shepp proved that the shortest path consists of no more than five segments, where in each segment u is either −1/rmin, 0 or 1/rmin and the car is moving either forward or backward [3]. The optimal path is one of 48 different path types. The Reeds–Shepp path can be used as a metric for the (x, y, θ) configuration of a car. The distance does not change if you swap the start and goal positions.

The Dubins car has only six possible path types [3]. Because it can only move forward, the Dubins path is never shorter than the Reeds–Shepp path. The Dubins path is not a metric, and often the distance changes if the start and goal positions are swapped [4].

This Reeds–Shepp implementation is based on the Python code [5].

Browser port of a Wolfram Demonstration. Drag the black locators to move the start and goal cars and the coloured ones to turn them (they stay 2 units from the car); positions are kept within ±13 × ±8, as in the original. "distance" is the original's norm of the (x, y, θ) difference. Arrow keys move a focused locator. All computation runs in your browser.
